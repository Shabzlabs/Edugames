About These Worksheets

The Spinner Graph Worksheets help students connect an interactive spinner game with paper-based probability and data practice. Students make a prediction, spin several times, record each result with tally marks, colour a simple bar graph, and compare which outcome happened most and least often.

This resource works well after students play Spinner Adventure, but it can also be used as a stand-alone math centre, small-group task, whole-class graphing activity, or quick review lesson.

What Students Practise

  • Making simple predictions before collecting data.
  • Recording spinner outcomes using tally marks.
  • Organizing results in a simple table.
  • Creating and reading a bar graph using a 0–10 scale.
  • Comparing results using words like most, least, more, fewer, likely, and unlikely.
  • Reflecting on whether the results matched their prediction.

Suggested Lesson Flow

  1. Introduce: Show students the spinner and discuss what outcomes are possible.
  2. Predict: Students choose which outcome they think will happen most often.
  3. Play: Students play Spinner Adventure or use a classroom spinner to complete a set number of spins.
  4. Record: Students use tally marks to track each result.
  5. Graph: Students colour the bar graph to match their data.
  6. Discuss: Students compare the most common and least common outcomes and explain whether their prediction was correct.

Differentiation Ideas

Extra Support

Model the first few spins together, provide a completed example of tally marks, or let students work with a partner. Students who need support can complete fewer spins or use stickers/counters before colouring the graph.

Extra Challenge

Ask students to complete more spins, compare two different spinner designs, write their own probability question, or explain why a larger number of spins may change the results.

Assessment Ideas

Teachers can use the completed worksheet as a quick check for data collection, graphing accuracy, and probability vocabulary. The reflection question helps show whether students can explain their results instead of only colouring the graph.
